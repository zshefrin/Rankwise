## Why med spa marketing doesn't look like a trade's

A roofer's customer has a leak and a deadline. A med spa's prospective client is comparing two or three providers over days or weeks, reading before-and-after photos, checking who's actually performing the treatment, and, more than almost any other category Rankwise works, caring about discretion. Nobody wants their name attached to a public review naming what procedure they had done, and a marketing program that pushes for reviews the way a plumber's would is tone-deaf to that reality even before it runs into the actual rules. This is a considered, high-ticket, comparison-shopped purchase, closer in behaviour to hiring a lawyer than to calling someone about a broken furnace.

## Who actually regulates what you can say

"Med spa" isn't one regulated category, it's three, depending on who's holding the needle, and the rules genuinely differ.

If a physician performs the treatment, the College of Physicians and Surgeons of BC's advertising standard applies: no unreasonable expectations, guarantees, or warranties about results; before-and-after photos need written patient consent (or must be public-domain, with stock photos disclosed); no comparison implying superiority over another regulated professional; and only a certified specialist can use the word "specialist," with "surgeon" barred for cosmetic advertising without surgical certification. Testimonials aren't banned outright the way they are in chiropractic, but fabricated or self-authored reviews are.

If a registered nurse or nurse practitioner performs the treatment under a medical director, there's currently no dedicated advertising standard from their own college. The constraint runs indirectly, through the supervising physician's rules and the drug-advertising rule below.

And regardless of who performs the treatment, Health Canada's Food and Drug Regulations restrict how a prescription drug can be advertised to the public. A med spa naming an injectable brand alongside any claim about what it does, "Botox smooths wrinkles in one treatment" is the textbook example, can cross into an illegal prescription-drug advertisement. It reads like completely normal service-page copy, which is exactly why it's the sharpest trap in this category. A proofreader skimming for "does this sound salesy" won't catch it.

## How clients actually search for a med spa

Search here skews toward the treatment or the brand name: "Botox [city]," "lip filler near me," "med spa consultation [neighbourhood]," comparison-shopped across a short list before booking, often starting with a Google or Instagram search rather than a referral. There's very little emergency search in this category. Almost every search is a considered one, which means the content that wins is the page that actually answers what a first-time client wants to know (who's performing it, what it costs, what the consultation involves) rather than a generic "look and feel your best" page.
