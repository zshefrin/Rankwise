# Chiropractic clinic marketing in Metro Vancouver

Yes, a chiropractic clinic's Google visibility in Metro Vancouver is genuinely winnable. But since April 2026 the province's regulator bans the exact tactic most local-marketing programs lead with: asking a patient to leave a review. Expect to pay $750-$1,250/month for a program built around your profile, citations, and content instead, with a written 90-day map-pack milestone attached before the first invoice.

## What actually decides who shows up first

Profile completeness carries more weight here than in most categories, precisely because the review-count lever is constrained. A Google Business Profile with every service listed, hours current, photos recent, and Q&A answered will out-rank a thinner profile that's simply accumulated more stars over time. Citation and NAP consistency, the same clinic name, address, and phone number matching everywhere Google can find them, is the second lever, and it's compliance-neutral: it works exactly the same way it would for any unregulated business. Content that answers a real question (do you treat sports injuries, do you direct-bill extended health, do you take same-day bookings) beats generic "we care about your spine" copy, the same way it does everywhere else. Here it's doing more of the ranking work because the review engine is throttled.

## The review rule that changes everything

As of April 1, 2026, the College of Complementary Health Professionals of BC's Professional Standard on Advertising and Marketing directly bans soliciting testimonials. Its own FAQ names "asking a patient to leave a review" and "providing a link or prompt to leave a review" as prohibited conduct. This is the sharpest compliance line across every category Rankwise works, and it isn't ambiguous. It's a plain-English ban, in force now. Rankwise's review-velocity engine, the review-request system run for trades, is fully disabled for chiropractic clients. What stays unaffected: any review a patient leaves on their own, unprompted, still counts and still helps your profile.

The same standard also bans efficacy guarantees, comparative or superiority language ("the best chiropractor in the city"), and the words "expert" or "specialist." There's no BC chiropractic specialist designation, so those words are off the table for every clinic, not just yours. What we run instead: GBP completeness, citation consistency, and content describing what you actually do, with no guarantee, comparison, or credential claim attached.

## How people actually search for a chiropractor

Chiropractic search skews condition-driven rather than emergency-driven: "chiropractor for lower back pain near me," "sports injury chiropractor [city]," "chiropractor that takes ICBC." These are searches triggered by a specific problem, often after a referral or after trying to wait it out. Extended-health coverage is a real filter; a profile and content answering "do you direct bill" wins searches that a generic "chiropractic care" page loses. There's a smaller acute-injury segment too (a fall, a sports injury) that behaves closer to emergency search, but most volume is considered, problem-specific, and plays out over a few days rather than a few minutes.

## Managing multi-practitioner clinics

Many Metro Vancouver chiropractic clinics run several practitioners under one business name, sometimes each with an individual Google profile in addition to the clinic's own. That's an operational detail worth getting right: the review-solicitation ban applies to every practitioner's marketing, individual or clinic-level, and a scattered set of under-managed personal profiles competing with the clinic's own listing can dilute the exact signals (consistent NAP, complete service listings) that carry the ranking weight here. Part of the monthly work is deciding, with you, whether individual practitioner profiles help or fragment your visibility, and managing them consistently either way.

**Can you ask my patients to leave Google reviews?**
No. CCHPBC's Advertising and Marketing standard explicitly bans soliciting testimonials as of April 2026, so review requests stay off for every chiropractic client, full stop.

**Do organic reviews still help if you're not soliciting them?**
Yes. The ban is on asking, not on receiving. Any review a patient leaves unprompted still counts toward your profile's ranking signal and still gets a professional response.

**Can you say I'm "the best chiropractor" in my city?**
No. Comparative or superiority language is banned under the same standard, along with the words "expert" and "specialist," since no BC chiropractic specialist designation exists.
