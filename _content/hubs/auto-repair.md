# Auto repair shop marketing in Metro Vancouver

An auto repair shop in Metro Vancouver should hire local-search help, but should go in with a longer timeline than most trades and a monthly spend of $750 to $1,250 aimed at review volume first. This is one of the harder map pack fields to move in the Lower Mainland, and the honest read on why is below, not a sales pitch built around it.

## Why this map pack is harder to crack than most trades

Auto repair is the second-hardest unregulated category Rankwise tracks for displacing a #1 result, behind only HVAC. Only 7.2% of tracked auto repair keywords currently have a top-ranked business with fewer than 30 reviews (as of 2026-07-30), compared to 33.3% for roofer or 24.0% for landscaper, where a third to a quarter of the field is genuinely open. Auto repair's incumbents are old, established, and review-heavy: the highest review count Google surfaces across this category's tracked cities is 38,000, and the metro-wide median business already sits at 173 reviews (as of 2026-07-09). A shop that's been open five years and asked every customer for a review can still be starting from behind a business with that kind of head start. This isn't a reason to skip marketing. It's a reason to set the 90-day milestone conservatively and measure early progress in review-count trajectory as much as in Map Pack position, because position tends to lag volume in a field this dense.

## How customers actually search for a mechanic

Two different searches drive this category, and neither behaves like an electrician's emergency call. The larger volume is routine and recurring: oil changes, brake jobs, pre-purchase inspections, seasonal tire swaps, searched by people who already have a shop they trust and are checking whether it's still the right one, or who just moved and are shopping cold. The smaller, higher-intent volume is the breakdown search: a check-engine light, a noise that wasn't there yesterday, a car that won't start. That searcher decides faster but still checks reviews before calling, because a bad repair on a car is an expensive thing to undo. Both searches reward the same signal in this category more than any other lever available: sustained, recent review volume. With a metro median already at 173 reviews, a thin or stale profile reads as a red flag to both searcher types rather than just an unknown quantity worth a chance.

Ticket size shifts how much that trust threshold matters. A routine oil change is low-stakes enough that a driver will often just try the closest shop with a decent star rating. A transmission rebuild, a major engine repair, or a diagnostic on a car that's been dying intermittently for weeks is a different decision entirely; the driver has usually been burned once already by a shop that guessed instead of diagnosed, and they read further into the review text itself, not just the star average, looking for other customers describing a similar repair done right. A shop that wants the higher-ticket work needs review content that names specific repairs, not just a pile of five-star ratings with no detail behind them.

## What the map pack looks like city by city

Google currently surfaces 310 auto repair businesses across the Metro Vancouver and Fraser Valley cities Rankwise tracks, and only 65% of them have a website, the lowest website rate of any trade category benchmarked. That's unusual for a field this review-heavy, and it suggests a real number of established shops are running almost entirely on GBP and word of mouth rather than a maintained site. City medians vary less here than in some other trades: Pitt Meadows sits at 115 reviews and Vancouver at 262, so the "easier" cities in this category are still a real climb, not an open door the way a thinner field like electrical offers. Unlike Ontario, Manitoba, or Quebec, BC does not license or provincially regulate repair shops themselves, which means there's no shop-licensing gate here at all. The whole competitive fight is review volume, GBP activity, and citation consistency, not credentials.

## What a shop's monthly marketing should actually look like

- A review-request system triggered at point of service, by SMS or email, because volume is the single biggest lever in this category
- Responses posted to every review, positive and negative, within a few business days; a shop with 173-plus reviews and no owner responses reads as unattended
- GBP posts covering routine seasonal work like tire changeovers and pre-winter inspections alongside emergency-repair keywords, since both searcher types land on the same profile
- Quarterly citation/NAP audits; with 65% of competitors lacking a website, GBP and directory listings carry more ranking weight here than the site does
- Monthly Map Pack tracking reported against named local competitors, with review-count trajectory tracked alongside position
